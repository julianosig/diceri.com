# Build a cloned-voice translation call stack

As of November 2026, the best cost/benefit for a two-party, link-joined call where each listener hears the other person translated in a voice like their own is a **streaming cascade**, not a single end-to-end model. The cascade is **Soniox `stt-rt-v6`** for speech-to-text (with AssemblyAI-STT-3.7-Realtime as the benchmarked challenger), a **Flash-class LLM** for translation, and **ElevenLabs Eleven v4 Turbo** (`eleven_v4_turbo`) for instant-clone streaming speech. Both of your initial picks hold up. "v4 Turbo" is a real, current, top-ranked model. AssemblyAI is a strong but pricier fallback, and the "Universal-Streaming" product you may have looked at was replaced by STT-3.7 on November 17. The cascade costs roughly **$0.04 per call-minute for both directions**. Our latency budget estimates **about 1–2 s from the end of a phrase to translated audio**. That is not measured, but it is consistent with the 2.7 s measured for an untuned small-LLM cascade. It also gives you real voice identity, captions, glossary control and any-to-any language pairs. The one end-to-end model worth A/B testing is Google's **Gemini 3.5 Live Translate**, which powers Google Meet's own translation and was measured at about 3 s. It is still in preview, and its voice carry-over is prosody-like rather than a guaranteed clone. On the media side, run **LiveKit** as the SFU, with a Rust "translator agent" that subscribes to each speaker's track and publishes one translated track per target language. axum stays the control plane. The repository should be a pnpm + Cargo monorepo pinned to **Node 26 LTS, pnpm 12, Rust 1.100 / edition 2024, SvelteKit 3, Svelte 5 runes, Tailwind 4 and TypeScript ~6 (not 7)**. Enforce it with lefthook, commitlint, lint-based size limits and nested AGENTS.md files. Three caveats apply. Three of the leading STT releases are under a month old and not yet independently benchmarked. No independent benchmark of Portuguese or Spanish streaming accuracy exists. No independent benchmark of cross-lingual voice cloning exists. Run a short in-house test before you commit.

## The pipeline is a cascade, and E2E models do not yet beat it

Two managed speech-to-speech models arrived in 2026, and both are tempting because one WebSocket replaces three vendors. **Gemini 3.5 Live Translate** (preview since June 9, 2026) auto-detects 70+ source languages. It carries the speaker's pitch, intonation and pacing into the output, and it costs about **$0.037/min** ([ChatForest](https://chatforest.com/builders-log/gemini-3-5-live-translate-speech-to-speech-70-languages-live-api-builder-guide/)). Google Meet now uses it for any pair among 63 languages ([Google Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/real-time-speech-translation-in-google-Meet-expands-to-63-languages-with-automatic-language-detection.html)). Google itself says voice replication "can be inconsistent", and a builder guide warns that it should not be treated as an identity-preserving clone ([AssemblyAI comparison](https://www.assemblyai.com/blog/best-real-time-speech-translation-apis)). It has no SLA, Live API sessions default to a 15-minute cap, and the context window holds about 68 minutes of audio. **OpenAI gpt-realtime-translate** ($0.034/min) supports only **13 output languages** and documents no voice preservation ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/advancing-voice-intelligence-with-new-models-in-the-api/)).

The only commercial head-to-head is small and vendor-run, so read it as directional. It measured Gemini at about **3.1 s** from speech end to finished audio, with a comprehension score of 4.93/5. OpenAI reached first audio in 711 ms, but its lag grew to **20.3 s on dense speech**, and it scored 4.53. A plain Whisper + GPT-4o-mini cascade finished in **2.7 s** with few erasures. When the speaker switched languages mid-clip, both E2E models stopped translating ([LiveLingo benchmark](https://www.livelingo.io/research/benchmark-2026)). The open-weight options cannot ship in a product today. Meta's SeamlessExpressive license forbids commercial use ([Meta Seamless License](https://ai.meta.com/resources/models-and-libraries/seamless-license/)). Kyutai's Hibiki-Zero has the best published speaker similarity (human MOS 70/100 versus 21 for Seamless), but it translates **only into English** ([arXiv 2602.11072](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11072v1)).

So the cascade wins on what this product needs. A tuned cascade is not slower than E2E after the speaker stops. It is the only route to a genuine consented clone in 90+ languages, and it produces text you can show as captions and correct with a glossary. Gemini 3.5 Live Translate earns a place as the **A/B challenger and a fallback path**, kept behind the same internal interface.

### Speech-to-text: Soniox v6 on price and speed, AssemblyAI 3.7 on proven accuracy

Streaming STT changed three times in November. Soniox shipped **stt-rt-v6** on November 3 ([Soniox](https://soniox.com/blog/soniox-stt-v6)), ElevenLabs shipped Scribe v3 Live on November 10, and AssemblyAI shipped **STT-3.7-Realtime** on November 17. AssemblyAI's legacy Universal-Streaming is now marked "replaced" ([AssemblyAI models](https://www.assemblyai.com/llms/models.md)). The most recent independent table (Coval, English only, 30 days to October 28) ranks **AssemblyAI Universal 3.6 Pro first at 2.2% WER**, 106 ms p50 time-to-final. Soniox v5 scored 4.8% at **37 ms** and **$0.12/hr**, and Deepgram Flux Multilingual scored 7.4% ([Coval](https://www.coval.ai/blog/best-speech-to-text-providers-in-2026-independent-benchmarks-and-how-to-choose/)). Soniox's accuracy swings sharply between benchmarks. Artificial Analysis measured it at 11.9% in June ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/articles/new-streaming-speech-to-text-benchmark-aa-wer-streaming)), so it is sensitive to the test set and must be checked on your own audio.

Two facts tip the primary choice to Soniox. First, it **includes real-time two-way translation, language ID and diarization at no extra charge**: `"type":"two_way"` with `language_a`/`language_b` streams translation tokens that start before the sentence ends ([Soniox docs](https://docs.soniox.com/stt/rt/real-time-translation)). Second, it costs about **$0.002 per participant-minute**, against **$0.005** for AssemblyAI 3.7-Realtime. AssemblyAI bills for the whole WebSocket session, idle time included, and has no streaming translation ([AssemblyAI pricing](https://www.assemblyai.com/pricing.md)). In this pipeline, a 1–2 point WER difference is largely absorbed by the MT step. Finalization speed and segment boundaries decide perceived lag. AssemblyAI's advantages are the strongest independent accuracy record, an EU endpoint at the same price, and a **3.7-Realtime-Agent** variant ($0.45/hr). Its vendor-stated figures are finalization 86 ms after speech ends and only 1% partial churn. That makes it the right challenger. Deepgram Flux Multilingual ($0.0078/min, 10 languages including PT) is the turn-detection specialist. Scribe v3 Live is worth including in the test only if you consolidate on ElevenLabs, because its price is unverified ($0.22 vs $0.39/hr). Skip OpenAI GPT-Realtime-Whisper ($0.017/min, 557 ms finalization). No vendor ships an official Rust SDK, but all of them use JSON plus binary PCM over WebSocket, so a thin `tokio-tungstenite` client behind a trait is enough.

### Translation: a Flash-class LLM that commits only stable segments

The strongest independent MT evidence is WMT25, where human evaluation ranked **Gemini 2.5 Pro first overall**. GPT-4.1 and Claude 4 formed a close second tier, and rankings varied by language pair ([WMT25 findings](https://aclanthology.org/2025.wmt-1.22.pdf)). Spoken segments are short, so latency matters more than the last point of quality. Use a current Flash/Flash-Lite or mini-class model, with the last two to four segments as context and a per-call glossary of names. The token cost is negligible: about 150 words per minute is well under $0.001/min. DeepL is a deterministic alternative for European pairs. Soniox's built-in translation can serve as a near-zero-latency draft for captions while your LLM produces the version that gets spoken. Compare the two on pt-BR↔EN/ES before deciding whether you need both. One rule is fixed: **never synthesize speech from re-translated partials**. In the LiveLingo test, Azure averaged 121 erasures per 120 s and the Google chain 353, and spoken audio cannot be retracted. Partials can go to captions. Only endpoint- or clause-committed text goes to TTS.

### Voice cloning: Eleven v4 Turbo leads, with Inworld and Cartesia as cheaper peers

ElevenLabs launched **Eleven v4 and v4 Turbo on September 28, 2026**. `eleven_v4_turbo` is the realtime variant. It supports 90+ languages, accepts streaming text input, and costs **$0.04/1K characters**, the same price as Flash v2.5, which it outclasses. Turbo v2.5 is deprecated ([ElevenLabs models](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/models); [ElevenLabs pricing](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing/api)). It leads Artificial Analysis's general quality board at about 1,340 Elo ([Fora Soft](https://www.forasoft.com/blog/article/real-time-voice-cloning-technology)). Independent time-to-first-audio, including the network, is **about 219 ms** ([Coval](https://benchmarks.coval.ai/providers/elevenlabs)). An instant clone needs about 10 s of audio and, according to the vendor, speaks other languages with a native accent ([Progressive Robot](https://www.progressiverobot.com/2026/09/28/eleven-v4-turbo-elevenlabs-tops-tts-arena/)).

The ranking is less clear-cut on the voice-cloning board itself. There, Alibaba Qwen-Audio-3.1-TTS-Plus (1210), HeyGen Voice and Speechify Simba 3.5 lead. **Eleven v4 Turbo (1158), Cartesia Sonic 3.6 (1153) and Inworld Realtime TTS-2 (1150) are statistically tied** ([Artificial Analysis Controlled Voice](https://artificialanalysis.ai/text-to-speech/leaderboard/controlled-voice)). That board is English-only, and no independent cross-lingual cloning benchmark exists for any API. This matters because your core case is a Portuguese reference voice speaking English, and the reverse.

| TTS option | ~$/output minute (list) | Clone reference | Why choose it |
|---|---|---|---|
| ElevenLabs v4 Turbo | ~$0.034 | ~10 s | Highest overall quality, 90+ languages, mature consent and delete API |
| Inworld Realtime TTS-2 | ~$0.021 (enterprise ~$0.004) | 3–15 s, free | Tied cloning Elo at about 60% of the price; non-English reportedly "experimental" |
| Cartesia Sonic 3.6 | ~$0.032–0.055 | ~10 s | 44 languages, multilingual clones, built-in watermark, enterprise self-host |
| Qwen3-TTS 1.7B / CosyVoice 3 (self-host) | ~$0.004–0.016 GPU-only | ~3 s | Data residency; trails top APIs by ~100 cloning Elo |

Prices convert $/1M characters at 850 characters per minute ([Fora Soft](https://www.forasoft.com/blog/article/real-time-voice-cloning-technology); [Inworld pricing](https://inworld.ai/pricing)). Gated providers are impractical for cloning arbitrary participants on the fly. OpenAI custom voices are limited to eligible customers, and Azure Personal Voice and Chirp 3 are allow-listed ([OpenAI custom voices](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/custom-voices); [Google Chirp 3 ICV](https://docs.cloud.google.com/text-to-speech/docs/chirp3-instant-custom-voice)). Gemini 3.8 Flash TTS needs a 30 s sample and is excluded in the EEA, UK and India ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/gemini-tts-3-8-flash-voice-design-cloning)). PlayHT has shut down. Start on v4 Turbo and keep Inworld as the cost lever. Qwen-Audio-3.1, HeyGen Voice Turbo and Simba 3.5 are on a watch list: their prices (~$0.003–0.016/min) and cloning scores are better, but their realtime APIs and consent flows were not verified.

### Consent is a product feature, not a footnote

Every cloning vendor requires the voice owner's consent. ElevenLabs requires an attestation on each API clone ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/voice-cloning-api)). **EU AI Act Article 50 transparency duties have applied since August 2, 2026**, and they cover cloned-voice audio, with fines up to €15M or 3% of turnover. Illinois BIPA treats voiceprints as biometrics ([Fora Soft](https://www.forasoft.com/blog/article/real-time-voice-cloning-technology)). In an anonymous room, that rules out silently cloning a participant. The workable flow is an opt-in "speak in my voice" toggle in the lobby. The participant reads a 10–15 s consent and liveness sentence, which doubles as the clone reference. The clone is session-scoped and deleted through the API when the call ends. Anyone who does not consent gets a stock voice. Session-scoped clones also keep you clear of per-account voice quotas, which were not verified for any vendor.

## LiveKit carries the media and a Rust agent does the translating

For a 1:1 call, perceived lag comes from provider endpointing and TTS, not from SFU forwarding. So choose the WebRTC stack for operational maturity: TURN, reconnection and client SDKs. That favors **self-hosted LiveKit** (or LiveKit Cloud). It has an embedded TURN server with integrated auth and automatic TCP/TURN fallback ([LiveKit deployment](https://docs.livekit.io/transport/self-hosting/deployment.md)). It also matches the translated-track model LiveKit itself uses, which publishes `{source_track_sid}-{language}` tracks with transcriptions attached ([LiveKit translation API](https://docs.livekit.io/reference/python/livekit/agents/beta/translation/index.html)). The architecture has three processes. **axum** creates rooms, mints short-lived, room-scoped LiveKit JWTs via `livekit-api`, and serves the static web build. **A Rust media agent**, built on the `livekit` crate 0.12.x, joins each room as a hidden participant. Its `NativeAudioStream` delivers decoded 48 kHz PCM, which it resamples with `rubato` to 16 kHz for STT. It publishes TTS PCM back through an audio source that encodes Opus ([livekit/rust-sdks](https://github.com/livekit/rust-sdks)). **The LiveKit server** handles SFU and TURN.

Inside the agent, each (speaker track, target language) pair gets its own tokio task graph. The stages are streaming STT, clause segmentation, MT and streaming TTS. They are joined by bounded channels that **drop the oldest frame rather than wait**, because a late audio frame is worthless. A `CancellationToken` handles language changes and barge-in. In a two-person call this means at most two translation sessions, and TTS runs once per target language, not once per listener.

| Option | Verdict |
|---|---|
| LiveKit SFU + Rust agent | **Recommended.** Mature SFU, TURN and JS SDK; libwebrtc-grade jitter and Opus in the agent. Costs an extra Go service and a pre-1.0, fast-moving Rust SDK that needs glibc. |
| str0m inside axum | Best pure-Rust option, sans-I/O and testable, but you build TURN, jitter buffer, codec, BWE policy and client glue yourself ([str0m](https://github.com/algesten/str0m)) |
| webrtc-rs 0.21 | Just reached a 1.0 RC after a sans-I/O rewrite; thin production record ([webrtc.rs](https://webrtc.rs/blog/index.html)) |
| Browser P2P + server tap | The server cannot see P2P media without a second uplink; keeping tracks in sync is hard |

There is one honest trade-off. LiveKit's turnkey translation module and its "Pipeline Translator" recipe (Deepgram → LLM → ElevenLabs) are **Python** ([LiveKit recipe](https://docs.livekit.io/reference/recipes/pipeline_translator.md)). A Rust agent therefore means reimplementing segmentation and turn handling. That work is feasible and keeps the stack in one language. A Python LiveKit Agents worker is the faster prototype if time-to-demo matters more. Avoid layering Pipecat over LiveKit for this design: one benchmark saw desynchronization with multiple active audio tracks ([Effloow](https://effloow.com/articles/pipecat-livekit-realtime-voice-latency-benchmark-2026)).

In the browser, there is **no official LiveKit Svelte SDK**. Wrap `livekit-client` 2.25 in a runes `RoomState` class, use `$state.raw` for SDK objects, and attach tracks with a Svelte attachment so adaptiveStream works ([LiveKit subscribe docs](https://docs.livekit.io/home/client/tracks/subscribe/)). Set `autoSubscribe: false` and subscribe each listener only to the translated track in their chosen language. Duck the original voice to about −15 to −20 dB so the speaker's presence remains. Keep `echoCancellation`, `noiseSuppression` and `autoGainControl` on. Play translated audio as a **WebRTC track rather than through `<audio>` or Web Audio**, so the browser's echo canceller has it as a reference. Gate playback behind `room.startAudio()` for autoplay policy.

For anonymous rooms, use 128-bit-plus random IDs (nanoid's 21-character default is ~126 bits), never a database key. Joins should be rate-limited per IP and per room. Tokens should live 5–10 minutes with audio/video-only publish grants. Enforce `max_participants = 2` server-side, set CORS to an explicit origin list, keep secrets in `secrecy::SecretString`, and expire rooms when the last participant leaves.

## Pinned versions and a three-root monorepo keep it orderly

The version landscape moved in ways that most tutorials and model training data miss. **Node 26 "Lithium" has been Active LTS since October 28, 2026** (v26.13.1). Node 24 entered maintenance on October 20 ([nodejs/Release](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nodejs/Release/main/schedule.json)). **SvelteKit 3.0 went stable on October 1** (now 3.4.0). It removes `svelte.config.js` (options move into the `sveltekit()` Vite plugin), replaces `$lib` with `#lib` declared in `package.json` `imports`, removes `$app/stores` in favor of `$app/state`, and **requires TypeScript 6 and Vite 8** ([SvelteKit 3 migration guide](https://svelte.dev/docs/kit/migrating-to-sveltekit-3)). TypeScript 7.0.2 is npm `latest`, but its native compiler drops the JS API that Svelte tooling relies on. Pin `typescript@~6.0.3` and use `svelte-check --tsgo` only as an optional speed-up ([svelte-check README](https://unpkg.com/svelte-check@4.7.8/README.md)). On the Rust side, **Rust 1.100** shipped November 12 ([Phoronix](https://phoronix.com/news/Rust-1.100-Released)). Edition 2024 is current, and no 2027 edition exists yet. axum is still on 0.8.x, while tower-http 0.7, sqlx 0.9 (MSRV 1.94), utoipa 6, reqwest 0.13 and jsonwebtoken 12 are all 2026 major bumps (crates.io API, November 25).

Sources: [npm registry](https://www.npmjs.com/) and [crates.io](https://crates.io/), both read on 2026-11-25. Use **adapter-static** with a `200.html` SPA fallback and `ssr = false` under the room route. The call experience is client-only WebRTC, so there is no Node process in production and no SSR state-leak bug class. axum serves the files. Generate one OpenAPI contract with utoipa and consume it with `openapi-typescript` + `openapi-fetch`. Export the WebSocket and data-channel message enums with ts-rs, and add a CI check that regeneration produces no diff. Verify that shadcn-svelte 1.8 writes `#lib` aliases before adopting it, because its docs still show `$lib` ([shadcn-svelte](https://shadcn-svelte.com/docs/installation/sveltekit)). Use Paraglide JS 3 for UI strings ([Paraglide](https://paraglidejs.com/sveltekit)).

The layout below combines the two research tracks. It uses three top-level roots so that the Cargo and pnpm workspace globs stay trivial. It also follows a hexagonal Rust split, so a vendor swap touches only one adapter, which matters in a market that changed three times this month.

├─ Cargo.toml  rust-toolchain.toml  rustfmt.toml  clippy.toml  deny.toml  .config/nextest.toml
├─ package.json  pnpm-workspace.yaml  .node-version  .editorconfig  renovate.json
├─ apps/
│  ├─ web/          (+ AGENTS.md)  SvelteKit: src/routes thin; src/lib/{components/ui, features/{room,call,media,translation,settings}, api, utils}
│  ├─ api/          (+ AGENTS.md)  axum bin: composition root, routes by feature, AppError -> RFC 9457, token minting, static serving
│  └─ media-agent/  (+ AGENTS.md)  LiveKit Rust SDK bin: per-speaker pipelines
├─ crates/  domain/ (pure types)  app/ (use cases + ports: SttProvider, Translator, TtsProvider)  infra/ (vendor adapters)  media/ (opus, resample, VAD, backpressure)  protocol/

Ship two container images: a slim `api` image (distroless works) and a glibc-based `media-agent` image, because libwebrtc rules out musl. Build both with cargo-chef, using the same Rust version in every stage ([cargo-chef](https://github.com/LukeMathWalker/cargo-chef)).

### Size limits, commits and AGENTS.md: enforce them in tooling

No standards body sets file- or function-size limits. ESLint's `max-lines-per-function` defaults to 50 and is off in `recommended` ([ESLint](https://eslint.org/docs/rules/max-lines-per-function)). The limits below are a practitioner judgment. What matters is that **lint fails the build** rather than a reviewer catching it.

| Target | Warn | Error | Enforced by |
|---|---|---|---|
| `.ts` file | 250 | 400 lines | ESLint `max-lines` (skip blanks and comments, exclude generated code) |
| `.svelte` component | 200 | 300 lines | ESLint `max-lines` override |
| TS function | 50 | 80 lines | `max-lines-per-function`, off for tests |
| TS complexity / depth / params | — | 12 / 4 / 4 | ESLint core rules |
| Rust file | 400 | 600 lines | CI script |
| Rust function | — | 80 lines | `clippy::too_many_lines` (`too-many-lines-threshold = 80`) |
| Items per folder | 10–12 | 15 | CI script |
| Line width | 100 | — | Prettier `printWidth`, rustfmt `max_width` |

Set Rust lints once in `[workspace.lints]`. Use `clippy::pedantic` at warn, and deny `unwrap_used`, `panic`, `todo`, `dbg_macro`, `print_stdout` and `await_holding_lock`. Add `unsafe_code = "forbid"` everywhere except, if needed, the media crate. CI runs `clippy -D warnings`, nextest plus doctests separately, `cargo deny` and `sqlx prepare --check`. Use thiserror in libraries and anyhow only at the binary edge.

Commits follow **Conventional Commits 1.0** ([conventionalcommits.org](https://www.conventionalcommits.org/en/v1.0.0/)), enforced by commitlint from a lefthook `commit-msg` hook ([lefthook](https://lefthook.dev/)). Scopes form a closed enum: `web`, `api`, `media`, `protocol`, `translation`, `infra`, `docs`, `deps`. Headers are at most 72 characters, imperative and lowercase. Each commit is atomic, builds, and passes `just check`. Generated code is committed together with its source change, and wire-protocol changes carry `!` plus a `BREAKING CHANGE:` footer. Prefer lefthook to husky + lint-staged in a polyglot repo: it uses one config, runs jobs in parallel, scopes jobs per app with `root:`, and needs no Node at hook time.

**AGENTS.md** is plain Markdown with no schema. The closest file wins, so put one at the root and one in each app. Name test commands explicitly, because agents run them, and symlink CLAUDE.md to it for compatibility ([agents.md](https://agents.md/)). The root file should cover a three-line product overview and an architecture sketch (web ↔ api ↔ LiveKit ↔ media-agent ↔ STT/MT/TTS). It should include the repo map, the exact pinned toolchain, and the `just setup | dev | check | test | fmt | gen | build` commands, with "run `just check` before finishing". It should also hold the size-limit table, feature-boundary rules, the "generated files are never hand-edited" rule, the security rules (unguessable IDs, api-only token minting, no secrets in `$app/env/public`, no cloning without consent), the ADR process (MADR 4.0 in `docs/decisions/`) ([MADR](https://adr.github.io/madr/)), and an English-only rule for code, docs and commits. Its most valuable section is a **banned-patterns list** of what agents trained on older code will try to write: `svelte.config.js`, `$lib`, `$app/stores`, `export let`, `$:`, `on:click`, `<slot>`, `createEventDispatcher`, `tailwind.config.js`, `@tailwind` directives, `.eslintrc`, TypeScript 7 as the main compiler, axum `/:id` routes, and pre-0.20 webrtc-rs or tokio-coupled examples. The nested `apps/web/AGENTS.md` covers runes patterns (`createContext`, `.svelte.ts` classes, `$effect` only for external sync, with cleanup), testing with fake media devices, and the autoplay gotcha. The `apps/api` and `apps/media-agent` files cover the error, state, shutdown (`CancellationToken` + `TaskTracker`), tracing and codegen conventions.

## Conclusion

Single-model "speech in, speech out" translation has not yet overtaken the cascade for voice identity. Gemini's version is shipping inside Google Meet, but it carries prosody rather than an identity clone, and the only commercial benchmark shows it taking longer after the speaker stops than a cascade does. The cascade also wins on modularity. Your build therefore comes down to three swappable adapters behind Rust traits plus a consent flow, and the market will keep reshuffling the vendors behind those traits. Measurement is the deciding factor, more than any single vendor choice. Before writing product code, spend one to two days running 30–60 minutes of your own pt-BR/EN/ES conversational audio through Soniox v6, AssemblyAI 3.7(-Agent), Flux Multilingual and Scribe v3 Live, and through v4 Turbo, Inworld TTS-2 and Cartesia cross-lingual clones. Score WER, time-to-final, false end-of-turns, ECAPA speaker similarity and blind listener MOS, then measure end-to-end latency in the browser. That test resolves the three things no public benchmark covers yet: Portuguese accuracy, cross-lingual clone likeness and true glass-to-glass lag. The pinned stack and enforced conventions matter for a related reason. Model training data, including that of coding agents, predates SvelteKit 3, Node 26 LTS and the 2026 crate majors. Without an explicit AGENTS.md and lint gates, the repository will drift back toward 2024-era code.
