# Default to one stack, route only proven exceptions

As of November 2026, the best multilingual combination is still one default provider per stage. **Soniox `stt-rt-v6`** handles speech-to-text, **Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite** with thinking set to minimal translates committed segments directly from X to Y, and **ElevenLabs `eleven_v4_turbo`** speaks the translation in a consented instant clone. On top of that sits a small routing table keyed on two things the app already knows at join time: the speaker's declared language and the listener's chosen language. This stack covers about **58 languages any-to-any**, close to Google Meet's 63. The cap comes from STT and TTS coverage, not from MT, because frontier LLMs now translate into Kazakh, Armenian and Belarusian at 77–84 on WMT26 human scores. Per-language routing earns its cost in only a few places. Two routes need no extra evidence: send low-resource, dialect and Traditional-Chinese targets to the stronger Gemini 3.5 Flash (same vendor, about $0.01 per speaking-minute), and send Cantonese, Zulu and Xhosa speech to AssemblyAI-STT-3.7, because Soniox does not list them. Two more routes should be switched on only if an in-house test confirms them: AssemblyAI for French, German, Dutch and Swedish STT, where it leads Soniox by 1.6–3 WER points, and Alibaba Qwen-Audio-3.1-TTS for Chinese, Japanese and Korean output, where every published cross-lingual cloning study favours the Qwen family but none has tested ElevenLabs v4. Three caveats apply. All multi-vendor per-language streaming STT data comes from one vendor's benchmark (AssemblyAI's). No benchmark measures speaker similarity for ElevenLabs v4. No benchmark measures latency per language for any TTS. The evaluation plan below is therefore part of the recommendation, not an optional extra. Compared with the earlier pt-BR/EN/ES report, the defaults survive. AssemblyAI moves from general fallback to a per-language route, ElevenLabs Scribe v3 Live becomes the general STT fallback, and Inworld's role as a cost lever shrinks to English and Spanish.

## Coverage tops out near 58 languages, and translation is not the bottleneck

A cascade supports a language only if all three stages support it, so its coverage is the intersection of three lists. Among production-grade streaming STT models, **Soniox v6 supports 60 languages** for transcription and built-in translation ([Soniox](https://docs.soniox.com/stt/concepts/supported-languages)). AssemblyAI-STT-3.7-Realtime supports 32. It misses about 13 of a practical top-35 list, including Polish, Ukrainian, Indonesian, Thai, Bengali, Tamil and Greek ([AssemblyAI](https://www.assemblyai.com/llms/models.md)). Three broader STT models exist, but none is GA yet or none has a confirmed list. **Gemini 3.5 Transcribe Live** claims 85+ languages but is in public preview ([Google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-3-5-transcribe/)). **MAI-Transcribe-2-Streaming** supports 60 languages but is a no-SLA preview ([testingcatalog](https://testingcatalog.com/microsoft-launches-mai-transcribe-2-streaming)). **Scribe v3 Live** was evaluated on 102 languages, although other sources say 73 or "90+" ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/blog/scribe-v3)). On the TTS side, ElevenLabs v4 and v4 Turbo share a flat **90+ language list** with no quality tiers ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/models)). Inworld TTS-2 publishes honest tiers: 15 "most extensively evaluated" languages, 79 "fully supported but more variable", and 376 best-effort with no text normalization ([Inworld](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/capabilities/multilingual)). Cartesia Sonic 3.6 covers 44 ([Cartesia](https://docs.cartesia.ai/build-with-cartesia/tts-models/latest)). Hume Octave 2 drops out entirely, because its TTS API was reportedly sunset on 13 November 2026 ([Versely](https://www.versely.studio/blog/hume-octave-2-emotional-tts-2026)).

Of Soniox's 60 languages, 58 also appear in ElevenLabs v4's published list. Albanian and Basque are not shown. The practical ceiling is therefore about 58 languages, against **63 any-to-any in Google Meet's Gemini-based translation** ([Google Workspace Updates](https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2026/10/real-time-speech-translation-in-google-Meet-expands-to-63-languages-with-automatic-language-detection.html)). Meet's breadth is matched, and the cascade adds a true clone.

MT no longer constrains coverage. At WMT26, Gemini 3.1 Pro landed in the top cluster on **15 of 23 pairs** and had the best average rank (4.8), and the three best systems overall were all built on Gemini. GPT-5.5 was close behind and won cs→de and cs→vi. Google Translate's classic NMT averaged rank 26.9 and scored **40.6 on zh→ja**, last in the field ([WMT26 findings](https://aclanthology.org/2026.wmt-main.48.pdf)). The real constraint sits at the edges. For roughly 25 languages that ElevenLabs can voice and Gemini can translate (Icelandic, Armenian, Georgian, Amharic, Nepali, Uzbek and others), no GA streaming STT in this stack can hear them.

The MT figures are WMT26 human cESA scores ([WMT26](https://aclanthology.org/2026.wmt-main.48.pdf)). For each language that is not Tier 1, the table names the component that limits it.

Two patterns stand out. First, the 23 Tier 1 languages are not "the European ones". They include Arabic, Hindi, Vietnamese, Thai and Indonesian, because ElevenLabs v4 closed the intelligibility gap that sank its predecessor. Multilingual v2 had **73% WER in Vietnamese and Thai** in 2025 ([MiniMax-Speech](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07916)), while v4 measures **0.61 CER in Vietnamese and 0.66 in Mandarin** ([arXiv 2610.18010](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.18010v2)). Second, almost every Tier 2 entry is labelled "unmeasured" rather than "measured weak". The tiering therefore reflects missing evidence as much as quality, and the evaluation plan exists to move languages between tiers.

## Each stage has a clear default, because per-language leaders trade places within a point or two

### Soniox v6 is the most even STT, not the most accurate one

On 18-language streaming FLEURS, the means are **Gemini 3.5 Live 6.0, Scribe v3 Live 6.8, Soniox v6 6.9 and AssemblyAI 3.7 7.5**, with Deepgram Nova-3 at 15.0 and OpenAI GPT Realtime at 11.8 far behind. On Common Voice the order flips: AssemblyAI comes first at 6.8 and Gemini drops to 8.9 ([AssemblyAI benchmarks](https://www.assemblyai.com/benchmarks)). That instability matters more than any single ranking. A one-point lead depends on the dataset, and both datasets are read speech, not conversation.

What separates providers is their **cliffs**. AssemblyAI collapses to 11–12% on Vietnamese and 11.2% on Arabic, against 5–7% for the leaders. Scribe v3 Live scores 9.3% on Hindi against 5.0–6.2%. Soniox has no cliff anywhere. It trails by a steady 0.5–2 points on Western European languages, and by 3 points on Swedish. It also placed first (0.74% semantic WER) in Pipecat's November realtime test of 23 systems, which is mostly English ([Soniox benchmarks](https://soniox.com/benchmarks)). Soniox also has the broadest GA list (60), the lowest price ($0.12/hr) and per-token language identification with language hints ([Soniox](https://docs.soniox.com/stt/concepts/language-identification)).

**Scribe v3 Live** becomes the general fallback. It has the second-best FLEURS mean, the second-best code-switching score (8.32% against AssemblyAI's 6.67% on EN↔European pairs), the broadest claimed list, and no cliff except Hindi. **Gemini 3.5 Live** posts the best numbers but is a preview. It also scored the worst conversational code-switching among the leaders, 40.7% on the Miami ES-EN set, and has no published price. It belongs on the watch list, not in production.

Language identification is the hidden multilingual risk. No source measures first-utterance misclassification, and Soniox documents that a language "may be temporarily misclassified" in real time. In a two-person call, the cheapest defence is to ask each participant for their language in the lobby (the same screen that captures the consent clip) and pass it as a hint or restriction, together with English. Open-set identification then becomes a choice between two or three languages.

### Gemini Flash-Lite translates every pair directly, and Flash backs it up where models diverge

The Gemini family led WMT25 (top or tied-top on 11 of 15 human-evaluated pairs) and WMT26 ([WMT25](https://aclanthology.org/2025.wmt-1.22.pdf); [WMT26](https://aclanthology.org/2026.wmt-main.48.pdf)). It led across high-resource targets, low-resource targets and non-English directions. It is therefore the safest family for any-to-any breadth. **Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite** costs $0.30/$2.50 per million tokens ([Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/gemini-3-5-flash-lite)), about **$0.002 per speaking-minute** for short segments with 2–4 segments of context. That cost is negligible. The trap is latency. Artificial Analysis lists Flash-Lite as a reasoning model with a **4.67 s TTFT that includes thinking**, and GPT-5.5 drops from 7.31 s to 1.91 s TTFT when reasoning is set to low ([AA comparison](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/gemini-3-5-flash-lite-vs-gpt-5-5-low)). Thinking must be minimal or off. No independent TTFT figure exists for that setting, so it must be measured.

Small models are where quality breaks. At WMT26, Gemma 4 4B scored 15.7 on en→et and 18.3 on en→be. Claude 4 trailed Gemini by 25–30 points on en→is and en→et at WMT25. No human evaluation exists for Flash-Lite, Haiku or nano-class models. That justifies one zero-integration route: send Tier 2 targets (Baltic, Balkan, Kazakh, Belarusian, Persian, Arabic dialects, zh-Hant) to **Gemini 3.5 Flash** ($1.50/$9, about $0.01 per speaking-minute; [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/May/19/gemini-35-flash/)).

The cross-vendor fallback for timeouts is **gpt-6-luna** ($0.10/$0.50) or **gpt-5.6-luna** ($0.20/$1.20) ([OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)), from the second-strongest WMT26 family. Claude Haiku 5.5 is equally cheap ($0.10/$0.50; [Decrypt](https://decrypt.co/380351/anthropic-launches-haiku-5-5-cheapest-fastest-claude-model)), but it has no multilingual evidence for long-tail targets.

**Translate directly, never through English.** Direct frontier-LLM systems top-scored every non-English WMT26 pair. Pivoting doubles serial latency and loses formality and honorifics. **Do not use DeepL as the primary MT.** It covers only about 36 languages ([DeepL](https://www.deepl.com/en/blog/vietnamese-thai-hebrew-launch)), which is fewer than the STT layer. **Use Soniox's built-in streaming translation for captions only.** It is fast and free with STT, but it streams word by word, publishes no quality figures, and gives untimestamped tokens that are not aligned to the source ([Soniox](https://docs.soniox.com/stt/rt/real-time-translation)).

### ElevenLabs v4 Turbo closed the intelligibility gap; speaker similarity is the open bet

In the only 2026 run that includes v4, Inworld TTS-2 and competitors (vendor-run by Fish Audio), **v4 has the lowest error rate of any system in 9 of 24 languages**. These include Czech, Finnish, Greek, Polish, Romanian and Vietnamese. **Inworld TTS-2 is worse than v4 in 19 of 24 languages**, with the largest gaps in Finnish, Czech, Turkish, Polish, Romanian, Arabic and Thai ([arXiv 2610.18010](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.18010v2)).

The open risk is speaker similarity, and the evidence there points the other way. Every 2025–2026 paper that measured SIM placed earlier ElevenLabs models well behind Chinese vendors. On the hard-zh subset, ElevenLabs v3 scored 50.3 against **78.7 for Qwen-Audio-3.0** ([arXiv 2607.23938](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.23938)). Qwen3-TTS beat Multilingual v2 on SIM in all 10 languages it tested ([arXiv 2601.15621](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15621v1)). For cross-lingual synthesis into Japanese and Korean, Qwen-Audio-3.0 averaged a 4.05% error rate across 12 CJK/English pairs. MiniMax-2.8 hit **30.7% on zh→ja**, and ElevenLabs v3 was in double digits on en→ja and zh→ja. None of these studies tested v4. Zero-shot cross-lingual SIM typically falls 0.05–0.20 below same-language SIM (MiniMax data: Chinese speakers into Finnish 0.55, into French 0.59; [arXiv 2505.07916](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07916)). For distant pairs, expect "sounds like a relative" rather than "sounds like the same person".

Vendors also make different accent choices, and that is a product decision. ElevenLabs claims v4 clones speak other languages "with a native accent" ([Progressive Robot](https://www.progressiverobot.com/2026/09/28/eleven-v4-turbo-elevenlabs-tops-tts-arena/)). Inworld defaults to a native accent and says openly that this costs some similarity ([Inworld](https://docs.inworld.ai/tts/capabilities/multilingual)). Cartesia clones reportedly keep the speaker's accent. All three tie on English cloning Elo (1158, 1153, 1150; [Artificial Analysis](https://artificialanalysis.ai/text-to-speech/leaderboard/controlled-voice)), so the cross-lingual test decides. Pricing is per character with no per-language rates ([Gradium](https://gradium.ai/content/best-multilingual-tts-apis-2026)). No vendor publishes time-to-first-audio per language. Clients should flush on CJK punctuation (。、！？) rather than on spaces, so that unspaced scripts do not stall the first chunk.

## Route per language only where a component falls off a cliff

The two-party topology makes routing cheaper than it looks. Each direction has exactly one (source, target) pair. The STT route is keyed on the speaker's declared language, and the MT and TTS routes on the listener's language. All of these are known before the first word, so a route is a lookup in a static config table behind the existing `SttProvider`, `Translator` and `TtsProvider` traits, not a runtime decision. A speaker's clone only has to exist at the TTS provider that serves the other participant's language, so routing CJK output to Alibaba creates one clone there instead of a duplicate. The consent text must still name every processor that can receive the voice sample. The real costs of a route are a second vendor contract, a second monitoring dimension and **different endpointing behaviour**, which changes how the call feels per language. That is why routes need evidence of a real cliff, not a one-point lead.

| Stage | Default (all languages) | Cross-vendor fallback | Route now (coverage or zero-cost) | Route only if in-house eval confirms | Do not use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **STT** | Soniox `stt-rt-v6`, $0.12/hr, with the declared language and English as `language_hints` | ElevenLabs Scribe v3 Live (broadest list; price unverified at $0.22–0.39/hr) | AssemblyAI-STT-3.7-Realtime for **yue, zu, xh** (Soniox does not list them) | AssemblyAI 3.7 for **sv** (6.2 vs 9.2), **fr** (4.7 vs 7.0), **de**, **nl**; Sarvam for **hi/bn**; Gemini 3.5 Live for **vi/hi** once GA | AssemblyAI as the general fallback (13 of the top 35 missing, cliffs on vi/ar); Deepgram (means of 12.5–15.0); OpenAI GPT Realtime (37% code-switching WER) |
| **MT** | Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, thinking minimal, direct X→Y, cached system prompt plus glossary plus 2–4 context segments | gpt-6-luna / gpt-5.6-luna on a TTFT miss of about 400–600 ms | Gemini 3.5 Flash for **Tier 2 targets** (Baltic, Balkan, kk, be, fa, Arabic dialects, zh-Hant) | Flash for any pair where it gains clearly on judged quality; Haiku 5.5 for high-resource pairs if its latency wins | English pivot by default; DeepL as primary; Google Translate NMT except for very-low-resource text |
| **Captions** | Soniox two-way streaming translation (free with STT) | LLM MT text after commit | — | — | Speaking any re-translated partial |
| **TTS** | ElevenLabs `eleven_v4_turbo`, $40 per 1M characters | Inworld TTS-2 ($25 per 1M; native accent by default) | v4 for **yue**, with a Mandarin output offer | Qwen-Audio-3.1-TTS for **zh/ja/ko** targets, and possibly **th/ms/tl**; Cartesia Sonic 3.6 as the Indic fallback | Hume (sunset); MiniMax 2.8 (zh→ja 30.7%); gated providers (Azure, Chirp 3, OpenAI); Speechify Simba 3.5 until cloned-voice languages on 3.5 are confirmed |

Routing barely moves the cost. The default path costs about **$0.04 per two-party call-minute**: 2 × $0.002 for STT, about $0.002 for MT and about $0.034 for one output-minute of v4 Turbo. An AssemblyAI STT route adds about $0.003 per routed participant-minute ($0.30/hr against $0.12/hr). Upgrading Tier 2 MT to Flash adds about $0.008. A Qwen TTS route at about $19 per 1M characters (AA list) would cut TTS cost roughly in half for those targets. Inworld fails over at about 60% of v4's price. Per-language routing is therefore an engineering and operations question, never a cost question.

## Fourteen languages and three matrices will confirm or overturn the table

The plan replaces the earlier pt-BR/EN/ES bake-off. It covers **14 test languages**: en, pt-BR, es, fr, de, sv, ru, ar, hi, zh, ja, ko, vi and th. Together they cover every Tier 1A family, the main candidate routes (sv/fr/de STT, CJK TTS), two STT cliffs (vi, ar) and one 1B language (th). Add id and he if those markets matter. The decision thresholds below are proposals derived from the observed gaps, not values from the sources.

**STT matrix (about 3 days).** Record 30–60 minutes of conversational speech per language through the product's own WebRTC path: Opus at 48 kHz, resampled to 16 kHz. Run Soniox v6, Scribe v3 Live, AssemblyAI 3.7 (where it supports the language) and Gemini 3.5 Live (as a reference). Measure WER/CER and semantic WER, first-utterance language-ID accuracy with and without hints, time-to-final at p50/p95, and false endpoint rate. **Turn on a route only where a challenger beats Soniox by more than 2 points and has no worse finalization latency.** On current data that rule would select sv, possibly fr, and nothing in Asia.

**MT matrix (about 2 days).** Choose about 25 directed pairs, including non-English ones such as pt↔ja, es↔zh, ar↔fr, hi↔en, ko↔ja and ru↔de, plus five Tier 2 targets. Feed them **real ASR output from the STT matrix**, not gold transcripts, because WMT shows speech is the second-hardest domain for exactly that reason. Compare Flash-Lite (thinking minimal), Flash, gpt-6-luna, Haiku 5.5 and Soniox's built-in translation. Score with a GEMBA-ESA-style LLM judge, as WMT26 did, plus native-speaker MQM spot checks on about 50 segments per pair. Also measure honorific and formality adherence for ja/ko/de/fr, and TTFT and full-segment time at p50/p95 from the deployment region. **Upgrade a pair to Flash only where it gains clearly and stays inside the roughly 600 ms segment budget.**

**Cross-lingual TTS matrix (about 3 days).** Capture 10–15 s consent-style references from 4 speakers (gender-balanced) in each of 11 languages, and synthesize them into each of the other 10. Run v4 Turbo and Inworld TTS-2 everywhere, add Qwen-Audio-3.1 for zh/ja/ko/th/ms/tl targets, and add Cartesia for Indic. Score ECAPA or WavLM speaker similarity (cross-lingual against same-language), back-transcribed CER, accent leakage with an L1-accent classifier, and a native-rater MOS plus a "same person?" rating. Measure time-to-first-audio per language from EU and US regions. Alibaba endpoints outside Singapore or Beijing are expected to add network round-trip time. **Turn on the Qwen CJK route only if speaker similarity improves by at least about 0.05 cosine, MOS is no worse, and time-to-first-audio stays within budget after network.**

Finish with a browser-measured, glass-to-glass latency run for one pair per tier. The routing table should live in configuration, with the eval rerun whenever a vendor ships a new model. Streaming STT changed three times in November 2026 alone.

## What changes from the earlier three-language recommendation

The three defaults (Soniox, a Flash-class LLM, v4 Turbo) all survive, but the reasons behind them change and so do the supporting roles.

**Soniox** was chosen earlier for price and bundled translation. It now also wins on breadth and evenness: it is the only GA 60-language streaming STT with no cliffs.

**AssemblyAI** was the general challenger and fallback. It is demoted to a per-language route, because it lacks 13 of the top 35 languages and falls to 11–12% WER on Vietnamese and Arabic. Scribe v3 Live takes over as the general fallback.

**MT** firms up from "a Flash-class LLM" to Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite with thinking minimal, Gemini Flash for Tier 2 targets, and a GPT luna fallback. Direct translation without pivoting is now an explicit rule, and DeepL drops out of the any-to-any design.

**v4 Turbo** is reinforced on intelligibility across 24 languages. Inworld's earlier role as the general cost lever narrows to English and Spanish, because it is worse elsewhere. Cartesia shifts from peer to Indic fallback. Qwen-Audio-3.1 moves from the watch list to a conditional CJK route.

**Gemini 3.5 Live Translate** remains an A/B challenger. Its 70+ languages now look like a possible stock-prosody path for Tier 3 languages that the cascade's STT cannot hear, although its exact language list has not been verified.

## Conclusion

The multilingual question turns out to be less about choosing the best vendor per language than about identifying which component fails first for each language. For about 23 languages, nothing fails, and one vendor per stage is right. In the middle band, the limit is mostly missing evidence. At the tail, the limit is speech recognition, because translation and synthesis are already ahead of it. This reverses the usual intuition that MT is the fragile stage. In 2026, the fragile stages are STT for low-resource and accented speech, and cloned-voice identity across distant languages.

Two practical consequences follow. First, the most valuable product investment is not a second vendor but a reliable language declaration in the lobby. It turns open-set language ID into a two-way choice, and it is the key every route depends on. Second, because the v4 speaker-similarity gap is the single largest unmeasured risk and the CJK route is its only remedy, the cross-lingual TTS matrix should run first. Its outcome decides whether this remains a single-vendor TTS product or a two-vendor one.
